## 1. What a good question is

A question is an invitation: to think, explain, sharpen, deepen, inform, investigate, connect.
A good question:
- is clearly formulated and born from an open, curious attitude;
- stays with the other person's story, not yours;
- sets thinking in motion;
- leads to clarification, new insight or a new perspective **for the person answering**.

A question is not: giving advice, checking a hypothesis, filling in, sharing an opinion, making a suggestion, or pinning someone down. Many everyday "questions" are one of these with a question mark attached. There are no bad questions, only badly made or badly used ones; a question is a tool, and the result depends on how you use it.

## 2. Why people ask bad questions

Knowing the cause helps you catch yourself.
1. **Biology.** Talking about yourself releases dopamine; asking does not. Hence the advice reflex, the help reflex, "I had that too", interrupting, and thinking about your answer while the other talks.
2. **Fear.** Of discomfort in the other, of your own discomfort, of conflict. So we swallow the real question and fill in assumptions instead.
3. **Scoring.** An opinion scores; a question shows you don't know. Doubt is not rewarded.
4. **Lost objectivity.** "That's my truth." Beliefs are tied to identity; questioning them feels like an attack, so people defend rather than think.
5. **Time.** We believe a good conversation costs time. It usually saves it: fewer misunderstandings, better decisions. Slow down to speed up.
6. **Competence.** Nobody taught us. Schools test knowledge; they don't train questioning.

## 3. The Socratic attitude

Technique without the attitude misfires. The attitude has these parts:
- **Wonder.** Choosing to see the ordinary as not self-evident. "Why does she react like that?" asked with curiosity instead of a label.
- **Curiosity, really wanting to know.** The other person is the expert on their own experience. Even when the story sounds familiar, you don't know their reasons (one person's "time" was the other's "comfort").
- **Courage.** The question you hesitate to ask is often the one that connects. Introduce it rather than swallow it.
- **Judge, and don't take the judgment seriously.** Judging is human and useful; the problem is attachment. Separate *judging* ("he delivers late") from *condemning* ("that is unacceptable"), and *observation* ("he sighed") from *interpretation* ("he can't be bothered"). Register the judgment, then withdraw it from the conversation. Ask yourself: is it true?
- **Perspective agility (perspectivistische lenigheid).** Stepping out of your own frame to follow the other's logic without "yes but". Test the internal consistency of their reasoning instead of rejecting it. Changing your mind is agility, not weakness.
- **Not-knowing, not doubt.** Doubt is anxious and wants an answer; not-knowing is calm and can wait. Decide to not know for five minutes, even when a decision is due.
- **Empathic zero (empathische nulstand).** Register emotion without confirming or denying it. No comforting, fixing, advising or sharing your story while investigating; each of these stops the other's thinking. This is distance *because* you care about their thinking, not indifference. You can choose empathy first and questions after; if you skip empathy, accept the irritation that may follow.
- **Socratic reflex.** When one person dissents from the group, the normal reflex is to fix them. The Socratic reflex gives the dissenter all the room; that is where the new view is.
- **Tolerate irritation.** No friction, no shine. Irritation after a question usually means the question landed.

## 4. Three listening positions

1. **I-position** ("what do I think of this?"): leads to advice, own stories, suggestive or steering questions. Questions from here are about you.
2. **You-position** ("what do you mean exactly?"): curious, Socratic. You want to understand their story, thinking and logic. Questions from here deepen. Train this one.
3. **We-position** ("how are we doing?"): the meta view. You notice you are going in circles, that every sentence starts with "yes but", that body language contradicts words. Useful for naming the dynamic.

**Superficial listening**: listen to the *form* of the language, not the content. Which kind of utterance (fact, claim, judgment, question, defence)? Which concepts? Which small words leak an agenda ("but", "then", "not yet", "just", "always", "all", "again")? Where is the contradiction, the reasoning error, the anecdote used as proof, the word-fog? What is not said? Listen in order *not* to understand too quickly, so you can ask what is behind the words.

Body language is language too: a sigh, a pause, a grimace, shifting in the chair are answers you can ask about. "I see you sigh. What does the sigh mean?"

## 5. Conditions before you ask

1. **Listen first** (section 4). A question that is really about you comes from half-listening.
2. **Take language seriously.** People do not choose words at random. "Where do I find focus?" differs from "How do I find focus?". Pick up the exact words.
3. **Ask permission.** Socrates always did. "Shall we dig into this together?" "Is it OK if I ask some critical questions?" This makes the inquiry a shared responsibility, tells the other that challenging questions are coming, and gives you an agreement to return to when it gets uncomfortable ("Do you want to go on?"). The other may say no. Without permission, probing feels like an interrogation.
4. **Slow down.** Twenty seconds of silence before a question produces a different, better question than the first one that came to mind. Take the time it takes so it takes less time.
5. **Tolerate frustration**, yours and theirs. It is a sign that something is moving, and it is material: "Why are you frustrated right now?"

## 6. Questions down and questions up

A judgment ("Marieke is a good manager") is an endpoint. The arguments behind it are hidden, often from the speaker too. Two directions open it up:

- **Down (concrete, facts):** what happened, literally. Who, what, where, when, how, how long, what was said word for word. The answer is evidence: "She is a good manager because she unblocks us within a day."
- **Up (abstract: arguments, norms, view of people):** "Why does unblocking fast make someone a good manager?" "What does X have to do with Y?" "Is someone who does X automatically Y?" The answer reveals a value: "Because a manager is there to remove obstacles."

Rules:
- When unsure, go **down**. People drift up into abstractions on their own; your job is to bring them back to the concrete case.
- **First down, then up.** Build the "film" until you can see it without filling in blanks. Then ask one question up. Then down again to test it against the case.
- Don't start with definitions. Concepts get their meaning when applied to a case; a dictionary definition collapses at the first real example.
- Alternate. Someone who answers "what makes this lazy?" with "doing nothing useful" gets the next question down: "Was she doing nothing useful here?"

## 7. Hot spot and core claim

In a story told with emotion, the emotion seems to be everywhere. It is not; it started at one moment. Find that moment (the **hot spot**, *hittepunt*) with questions down: "At which exact moment did you think: what is he doing?" Pin it to the second.

Then form the **core claim**:
> When ... (hot spot) ... I did / thought / felt ... because ...

Everything after "because" is the material for questions up. Example: "When he said 'I'll just go first, I only have one thing' and stepped in front, I felt anger, because it is rude to push in without waiting for a reaction." Now you can ask what makes it rude, whether waiting for a reaction is always required, whether he could have had reasons.

Summarize the film in the other's **exact words**: no paraphrase, no new concepts. "Is that right? Is anything missing?" The teller usually notices gaps themselves.

## 8. Question pitfalls catalogue

Flag by name. Most people fall into several a day.

| Pitfall | What it is | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Disguised statement** | An opinion with a question mark: "Don't you also think the plan is too expensive?" | Say the opinion plainly, then ask an open question. |
| **Comma-idiot question** (komma-sukkelvraag) | You could end it with ", idiot": "Haven't you sent that report yet?" Often negative form + "yet/again/still". | State what bothers you; then ask what happened. |
| **But-question** (maarvraag) | Starts with "but". Leaks: "I already have an opinion on this." | Delete "but". |
| **Cocktail question** | Two or more questions in one breath. The other picks the easiest. | One question. Park the rest. |
| **Vague question** | The other must guess your meaning of a concept: "Is it a lot of work?" | Make it concrete: "How many days do you estimate?" |
| **False either/or** | Two options offered where more exist: "Tuesday or Wednesday?" | Make it open: "When?" |
| **Not self-contained** (half-baked) | "What do you mean?" after a long story; unclear what it refers to. | Add the referent: "What do you mean by 'not robust'?" |
| **Too long** | A long introduction before the question. It smuggles your assumptions in and becomes a monologue. | Cut to the question. |
| **Why as punishment** | "Why didn't you do X?" is a reproach. | See section 9. |
| **Hidden advice** | "Have you tried talking to her?" | Hold it. Ask what they have considered. |
| **Own experience** | "Did you also have ...?" | Stay with their story. |
| **Paraphrase / new concept** | Replacing their words with yours. | Echo their exact words. |
| **Hypothesis check** | "Is it because you're afraid of the deadline?" | Ask down: "What happened just before?" |
| **Interpretation stated as fact** | "You're clearly annoyed." | Name the observation and ask: "You crossed your arms. What's happening?" |

Small words that leak an agenda: *but, then, not yet, just, always, all, again, actually, surely, also, of course.*

## 9. Open, closed and "why"

**Classify by structure, not by the answer you expect.**
- Open question: begins with who, what, where, which, when, how, what for.
- Closed question: begins with a verb ("Is", "Do", "Did", "Can").
- Begins with an article ("The reports were ...?"): not a question at all; rhetorical.

**Closed questions are not forbidden.** They are the right tool to check a fact, to bring structure to a rambling or emotional story, and to force a position ("Do you think we should stop? Yes or no, then your argument."). They backfire with a colleague who is reluctant to share. A verb-first question that is not a pure check is probably suggestive, a hypothesis check or rhetorical; if you actually want to say something, say it.

**"Why" is essential, with a leaflet.** Without "why" there is no new insight; ask it often. Its side effect: people hear it as a demand for justification, because we misuse it as a reproach ("Why are you working late *again*?"). So:
- In a warm, safe relationship, or when the other expects critical questions (you asked permission): use "why".
- Otherwise soften the form, not the intent: "What is the reason that ...", "What made you ...", "How did you arrive at ...".
- "Why" looks for one cause. For complex matters prefer "how": "How did it come about that ...?"

**"Tell me..."** is the universal opener for a short or grumbling colleague. It opens a valve without steering.

## 10. Probing and confronting

**Probing (doorvragen)** deepens what is already on the table; it adds no new topics. It needs distance from the content and from your own view. Tools:
- **Question the self-evident.** "Teachers just complain." → "What do they do that you call complaining?" "What makes it unjustified?"
- **Echo question.** Repeat their exact words, no paraphrase. "What was 'a whole hassle again'?" "What makes Kris 'such an idiot'?" One word also works: "All?" "Always?" It feels like parroting to you; to the other it feels like being heard.
- **Two directions.** First *their* direction until you understand it (understanding is not agreeing): "How do you know that for sure?" "What argument do you have?" "What do you base that on?" Only then the *opposite* direction: "Does everyone see it this way?" "What would someone say who disagrees?" "Could it be otherwise?"
- **Concepts.** Find the one-word idea under the claim (autonomy, control, fairness, ownership, quality). Two people who disagree usually hold opposite concepts. Ask about the edge: "Where does autonomy end and control begin?"
- **Suppose-that question.** After the view is understood, stretch it with their own words: "Suppose it had been your story that slipped; would you call that negligent too?" A fast, surprised answer often exposes a hidden norm the person did not know they held.

**Confronting = giving back.** Nothing more. Mirror what you heard so the other can think about it. Not: standing above, correcting, winning. Use it when someone is unclear, says much and little, contradicts themselves, or makes a reasoning error. You need little content knowledge; the more naive, the better.
- Echo one word: "All?"
- Ask naively for explanation: "How does that work exactly, that X leads to Y?"
- Name a non-answer and repeat: "That's not an answer to the question. You said what it relates to. What *is* taking responsibility?"
- Cut the fog: "What are you actually saying?"
Do not confront when you want to win, are irritated, or don't care. Then it becomes a reproach and you create the conflict you feared.

**Domino stones.** Answers should fit questions by structure. A yes/no question gets yes or no first, then arguments. If the answer does not fit, repeat the question calmly instead of pointing out that they didn't listen. For depth, you do the same: take position first, then nuance.

## 11. What to expect

- **Elenchus** (refutation, perplexity): the moment someone notices that what they were sure of does not hold. Signals: "you're making it complicated", "that's obvious", "that's just how it is", "it's only words". These mean you are close. Stay calm, keep asking ("Why is it obvious?"), or return to the agreement ("Do you want to continue?"). Shame is possible; don't rescue with empathy, don't push either. Socrates called this clearing the attic: only after the rubbish is on the table is there room for something that holds.
- **Aporia**: "I honestly don't know anymore." Not a failure; the start of real inquiry. From here comes a new question or a better-founded position.
- **Irritation**: at your calm, your questions, your refusal to agree or disagree ("can't you just have a normal conversation?"). Choose: empathy first, then continue; or persist and accept it. Never take it personally.
- **Energy**: choose your battles. You don't owe everyone a Socratic inquiry. But five minutes without expectations with the person who thinks least like you often pays.

## 12. Consensus, and bringing in your own view

**Strive for consensus** means keep searching: for overlap, for difference, for whether the difference is bridgeable. It is not compromise, not "agree to disagree", and "that's your truth / you're entitled to your opinion" kills the inquiry. Consensus is often found one level up ("stop helping when helping is no longer helping"). Reaching it matters less than keeping the search alive.

Feelings and opinions ("I feel that ...", "I think that ...") are not claims about reality. You can only note them. Ask for the claim behind them: "Ibrahim acted fairly here" can be investigated; "I feel Ibrahim acted fairly" cannot.

**Your own view: build a bridge.** First understand, then be understood. A bridge is as strong as both banks, so the other's side must be fully explored first. Then wait for "what do you think?" or make the bridge yourself:
- "I have some ideas on this too. Shall I put them on the table?"
- "Interesting position. I don't agree on every point. Shall I tell you how I see it?"
The bridge prepares the other's head to receive. Then speak briefly and to the point; position first, arguments after.
