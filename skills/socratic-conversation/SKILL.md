# Socratic conversation

Goal: a one-minute plan for a conversation in which the user first understands the colleague's thinking, then brings in their own view, instead of two monologues laid side by side.

This is step 3 of a set of three. For a single reply use `socratic-reply`; to audit a drafted question use `socratic-question-check`. The method is in `references/socratic-method.md`: read sections 3 (attitude), 5 (conditions), 6–7 (down/up, hot spot), 10 (probing, confronting) and 11–12 (what to expect, consensus, bridge) before planning. For a debrief, section 8 (pitfalls) as well.

## Input
The situation: who, what has been said so far, what the user wants (understand, decide, resolve a disagreement, test a claim, bring their own view). Output in the language the conversation will be held in. Short sentences.

## Steps (preparation)

1. **Attitude check, briefly.** Name the one or two attitude points that matter most here: curiosity (the colleague is the expert on their experience), not-knowing for five minutes even when a decision is due, judging without attaching, empathic zero, the Socratic reflex when the colleague is the dissenter, courage for the question the user is avoiding. Skip the rest.

2. **One question, one case.** Formulate the central question (not Googleable: "What is good collaboration in this team?", "When is a review strict enough?") and pick one concrete, real example to anchor on. No definitions first; concepts get meaning when applied to the case.

3. **Permission line.** In the user's own words: "Shall we dig into this together?" / "Is it OK if I ask some critical questions about this?" Note the fallback it buys: "Do you want to go on?"

4. **Down first.** Four to six fact questions that build the film: who, what, where, when, what was said word for word, what happened next. Plan to summarize in the colleague's exact words: "Is that right? Is anything missing?"

5. **Hot spot and core claim.** The question that pins the moment ("At which exact moment did you think ...?") and the template to fill: *When ... I did/thought/felt ... because ...*

6. **Up.** Three or four questions aimed at what comes after "because" and at the central concept: "What does X have to do with Y?" "Is someone who does X automatically Y?" "Where does X end and Y begin?" First in the colleague's own direction ("How do you know?", "What do you base that on?") until understood; only then the opposite direction ("Does everyone see it this way?", "Could it be otherwise?").

7. **Confrontation and suppose-that, with conditions.** At most two: an echo of one word ("All?"), a naive request for explanation, naming a non-answer and repeating the question, or a suppose-that in the colleague's words. State when to use each and when not (never to win, never when irritated).

8. **Bridge.** The sentence that brings in the user's view ("I have some ideas on this too. Shall I put them on the table?") and that view in one or two sentences, position first.

9. **Watch for.** Expected elenchus signals ("that's obvious", "you're making it complicated"), likely irritation at the question-only mode, and the agreed fallback. Remind: domino stones, so a yes/no question gets yes or no first; strive for consensus, never settle for "that's your truth"; slow down, twenty seconds of silence is allowed.

## Output (preparation)

```
**Core question & case**
**Opening** – permission line
**Questions down** – 4–6
**Hot spot & core claim** – the pinning question + template
**Questions up** – 3–4, own direction first, then opposite
**Confront / suppose-that** – ≤2, each with its condition
**Bridge** – sentence + the user's view in 1–2 lines
**Watch for** – signals and fallback
```

## Debrief
When the user reports how a conversation went: mark where they slipped into the I-position, advice, paraphrase, cocktail questions or definitions-first; name the hot spot and concepts found or missed; note whether the bridge was built before the view came in; give two questions they can still send as follow-up. No grades, no scores.

## Example (abridged)

Input: *"1:1 tomorrow with our PO. He keeps saying the team 'doesn't take ownership'. I think he means we don't say yes to his deadlines. I want to understand what he means before I push back."*

**Core question & case** – What is ownership in this team? Case: the last time he said it (which sprint, which story, what happened).
**Opening** – "Je zegt vaker dat het team geen ownership neemt. Ik wil dat echt begrijpen. Mag ik er een paar vragen over stellen?"
**Questions down** – "Wanneer zei je dat voor het laatst?" "Wat gebeurde er vlak daarvoor?" "Wie zei wat, letterlijk?" "Wat had je op dat moment verwacht dat het team deed?"
**Hot spot & core claim** – "Op welk moment precies dacht je: hier ontbreekt ownership?" → *Toen ... dacht ik ... want ...*
**Questions up** – "Wat heeft dat gedrag met ownership te maken?" "Is iemand die een deadline terugduwt automatisch iemand zonder ownership?" "Waar houdt ownership op en begint commitment aan een datum?" Then: "Hoe zou het team dit zelf omschrijven?"
**Confront / suppose-that** – Echo "het team?" when he generalises; use only if he says "altijd" or "niemand". Suppose-that: "Stel dat het team dezelfde deadline zelf had voorgesteld, was dat dan ownership geweest?" Only after his view is clear.
**Bridge** – "Ik heb hier ook een beeld bij. Zal ik dat naast het jouwe leggen?" View: "Ik denk dat we ownership tonen door nee te zeggen tegen data die we niet kunnen halen. Ik hoor dat jij het anders ziet."
**Watch for** – "Dat is toch duidelijk" means you are close; stay calm. If he gets irritated by the questions, name it: "Ik merk dat je zucht. Wil je doorgaan?"
